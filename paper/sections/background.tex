\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Stabilizer codes~\cite{gottesman1997stabilizer} track Pauli errors as binary vectors under syndrome measurement without collapsing the logical state, which is what makes classical decoding of quantum errors tractable at all. The three codes considered here (a 3-qubit repetition code, the Shor code~\cite{shor1995scheme}, and the Steane code~\cite{steane1996error}) are all distance-3 stabilizer codes, historically among the first shown to correct an arbitrary single-qubit error. The Shor code has parameters \([[9,1,3]]\); the Steane code has \([[7,1,3]]\): nine or seven physical qubits encoding one logical qubit with minimum distance three. Real-time decoding latency requirements for superconducting qubits have been analysed by Fowler et al.~\cite{fowler2012surface}; the decoder must complete within the syndrome round time, on the order of 500\,ns to 2\,\textmu{}s depending on the platform.

FPGA and ASIC decoder implementations exist across a range of code distances and design points. Das et al.~\cite{das2022afs} implemented a full syndrome-to-correction pipeline for the surface code on an Intel Stratix~10 FPGA. Liyanage et al.~\cite{liyanage2023scalable} report a distributed union-find decoder, ``Helios,'' on a Xilinx VCU129 FPGA scaling to distance 21 with a measured 11.5\,ns average decode time per measurement round under phenomenological noise -- to our knowledge the most directly comparable measured FPGA decoder result to the work described here, and the correct citation for a paper this manuscript's own bibliography previously misattributed (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Ryan-Anderson et al.~\cite{ryan2021realization} demonstrated closed-loop, hardware-measured real-time QEC on a trapped-ion system, which is the actual precedent for the claim this manuscript's title previously made and does not itself substantiate. Riverlane has reported FPGA and ASIC hardware decoders operating below one microsecond and, for a Collision Clustering decoder, as low as 810\,ns (FPGA, 881-qubit surface code) and 240\,ns (ASIC, 1057-qubit surface code). Google Quantum AI demonstrated a real-time decoder sustaining a distance-5 surface code below the error-correction threshold with an average decode latency of approximately 63\,\textmu{}s, on real superconducting hardware, in closed loop. A comparison table against these and other results, including where each figure is measured versus estimated and whether the system is closed-loop, is given in the accompanying response materials rather than reproduced in full here.

The present work differs from all of the above in scope: it targets distance-3 codes specifically, on a general-purpose FPGA accelerator card rather than a purpose-built decoder ASIC or a code-agnostic scalable architecture, and it reports HLS-level synthesis estimates rather than hardware measurements. Its contribution is not decoder novelty at this distance -- the Shor and Steane decoders reduce to small lookup tables and are not expected to be architecturally interesting -- but a measured accounting of interface cost that the larger, more novel systems above do not typically report in comparable form.

Stabilizer codes reduce to Boolean logic in a specific sense worth stating once: a Pauli error on \(n\) qubits is fully described by two binary vectors \(x_{\rm err}, z_{\rm err} \in \mathbb{F}_2^n\) (with a \(Y\) error setting both bits), the syndrome is a GF(2) matrix-vector product against the stabilizer generators' parity-check matrix, and decoding is a lookup or graph search over that syndrome. Appendix~\ref{app:reconstruction} gives the full derivation for readers who want it; it is not repeated in the main text because it is textbook material and this paper's contribution lies elsewhere.
