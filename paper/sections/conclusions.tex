\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

We set out to measure three small QEC decoder kernels on a commodity FPGA card and report their hardware latency and throughput. What we can report is a synthesis-level characterisation, for one kernel now confirmed on real hardware, and an honest account of what remains estimated for the other two. The decode logic itself is small and fast, and the specific interface required to read a result back without elevated host privileges costs 35 to 70 times the decoder's own pipeline depth at the HLS-estimate stage -- and we built and tested that fix, not just estimated it: the \texttt{m\_axi}-fixed Shor kernel passes its full self-test on a live Alveo U55C with a genuine hardware read-back, its post-route resource and timing reports are real, and a preliminary latency measurement confirms that host dispatch, not kernel logic, is where the real cost sits, by roughly three orders of magnitude.

Alongside it, this revision produced real Monte Carlo evidence, verified against an independently written reference decoder, that substantially revises this line of work's central logical-error-rate claim for the Shor code: the measured rate under IID-depolarising noise is roughly an order of magnitude higher than previously reported, a gap confirmed well outside statistical uncertainty at \(10^7\) shots. It also produced the first circuit-level, multi-round result associated with this work, for the repetition code, and a corrected scalability analysis showing precisely where LUT-based decoding stops being viable.

One kernel's hardware verification does not make this a hardware-characterised system. The repetition-code and Steane kernels remain HLS-ESTIMATE throughout, the Shor kernel's own hardware coverage is one self-test and one non-rigorous latency measurement rather than the exhaustive sweep and tail-resolving timing a real-time claim would need, and every number in this paper is structured so that closing those gaps can slot directly into the same tables and figures without requiring a rewrite. What has changed, concretely, from where this campaign started is that the central open question -- does the fix the manuscript's own limitations analysis proposed actually work -- now has a real, hardware-measured answer, and the answer is yes.
